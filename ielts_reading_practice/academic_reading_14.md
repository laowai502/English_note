Rethinking the City
For more than a century, the modern city was designed around the private automobile. Roads were widened, neighbourhoods were cleared, and public spaces were sacrificed to make room for moving and parked vehicles. This model delivered undeniable benefits in terms of personal mobility and economic growth, but it also produced congestion, pollution, and a profound sense of alienation from the urban environment. In recent years, a growing movement has sought to challenge the dominance of the car, not merely by improving public transport, but by fundamentally rethinking how urban space is allocated and who it is for.

One of the most influential concepts to emerge from this movement is the “15-minute city.” The idea is deceptively simple: residents should be able to meet most of their daily needs—work, education, shopping, healthcare, and leisure—within a short walk or bicycle ride from their homes. Crucially, this is not a call for self-contained villages or an end to movement, but rather a strategy to reduce the necessity of long-distance travel. Proponents argue that by dispersing services and encouraging mixed-use development, cities can become more resilient, healthier, and less dependent on fossil fuels.

Yet the implementation of such ideas has often proven contentious. In some cities, proposals to restrict car access to certain streets have provoked fierce opposition from residents and businesses. Opponents argue that restricting vehicles harms local commerce and disproportionately affects elderly and disabled people who rely on cars for mobility. In a few cases, the debate has become politically charged, with conspiracy theories circulating that 15-minute city policies are a pretext for restricting personal freedoms. Urban planners respond that such fears are unfounded and that well-designed policies are intended to expand access, not constrain it.

The evidence on the benefits of reducing car dependence is substantial. Studies from cities that have pedestrianised central areas or introduced low-traffic neighbourhoods consistently report improvements in air quality, reductions in traffic accidents, and increases in walking and cycling. Retailers often overestimate the proportion of their customers who arrive by car; in practice, pedestrians and cyclists tend to visit more frequently and spend more over time. Moreover, the health benefits of active travel are well documented, with regular walking and cycling associated with lower rates of cardiovascular disease, obesity, and mental distress.

At the same time, researchers caution against treating the 15-minute city as a universal template. The concept assumes a degree of population density that is absent in many suburban and rural areas, where distances between homes and services are simply too great to be traversed on foot or by bicycle. Nor does it easily accommodate the needs of shift workers, carers, or those whose employment requires travel across large urban regions. Without substantial investment in public transport and affordable housing, the 15-minute city risks becoming an amenity for the privileged rather than a model for equitable urban development.

What is clear is that the question is no longer whether cities should reduce their dependence on cars, but how quickly and how fairly this transition can be achieved. The challenge is as much political as technical. It requires not only redesigning streets and reallocating space, but also building public trust, resisting misinformation, and ensuring that the benefits of urban transformation are shared across all sections of society. The city of the future may look less like a network of highways and more like a patchwork of connected, self-sufficient neighbourhoods, but getting there will demand patience, persuasion, and a willingness to confront deeply embedded assumptions about what a city should be.
