The Battery Revolution
For much of the twentieth century, the humble battery was an afterthought—a convenience that powered torches, toys, and portable radios, but rarely figured in grand visions of the future. That is no longer the case. Batteries are now central to global efforts to decarbonise energy systems and to the future of transport, and their development has become a matter of intense scientific, industrial, and geopolitical competition. The transformation of battery technology from a mundane component into a strategic technology has been rapid, but it is not without complications.

The dominant technology in portable electronics and electric vehicles remains the lithium-ion cell. Its appeal lies in its high energy density—the amount of energy it can store per unit of weight—and its ability to be recharged many times. However, lithium-ion batteries have well-documented drawbacks. They depend on a limited number of materials, including cobalt and nickel, whose extraction is frequently linked to severe environmental degradation and, in some regions, to exploitative labour practices. The supply chains for these minerals are also highly concentrated geographically, leaving manufacturers vulnerable to political instability and export restrictions.

These concerns have intensified the search for alternative chemistries. Sodium-ion batteries, for instance, have attracted growing interest because sodium is abundant, cheap, and widely distributed. Although sodium-ion cells currently store less energy than their lithium-ion counterparts, they perform better in cold conditions and are less prone to catching fire. Researchers are also exploring solid-state batteries, which replace the flammable liquid electrolyte of conventional cells with a solid material. If successfully commercialised, solid-state designs could offer significant improvements in safety and energy density, though major manufacturing challenges remain.

At the same time, the question of what happens to batteries at the end of their useful life has become increasingly urgent. Recycling rates for lithium-ion batteries remain low in most parts of the world. Many discarded cells end up in landfill, where they pose fire risks, or are exported to countries with inadequate environmental regulations. Proponents of a circular economy argue that battery design must be rethought from the outset so that components can be recovered and reused with minimal energy input. Some manufacturers have begun to embrace this principle, designing cells that are easier to disassemble and using recovered materials in new production.

Public attention, however, has often focused not on the technical details of battery chemistry but on the more visible problem of charging infrastructure. Drivers considering the switch to electric vehicles frequently cite "range anxiety"—the fear of running out of power before reaching a charger—as a major obstacle. Yet surveys suggest that most daily journeys fall well within the range of even modest electric vehicles, and that the perceived need for ubiquitous charging stations may be exaggerated. For the majority of urban users, home or workplace charging could meet nearly all their needs.

The geopolitics of batteries cannot be ignored. Control over the minerals, processing capacity, and manufacturing expertise required for advanced batteries has become a source of international leverage. China currently dominates the production of battery-grade chemicals and the assembly of battery cells, a position that has prompted other nations to invest heavily in domestic capacity. Some governments have introduced policies requiring that a certain proportion of battery components be sourced locally, while others have funded research into chemistries that reduce dependence on scarce materials. The race for battery supremacy is thus not merely commercial; it is also a contest over who will shape the energy systems of the future.

Ultimately, the battery revolution embodies the tensions that characterise much of the energy transition. It promises cleaner transport and more resilient electricity grids, yet it also creates new environmental burdens and new forms of dependency. The challenge for societies is not simply to produce more batteries, but to produce better ones—and to ensure that the benefits are not offset by the hidden costs of their production and disposal.
