The First Americans: Rethinking the Route
For decades, the standard account of how humans first reached the Americas seemed settled. According to this model, people crossed a land bridge known as Beringia, which connected Siberia to Alaska during the last Ice Age, roughly 15,000 years ago. They then moved south through a corridor between two massive ice sheets, eventually spreading across the continents. This narrative, often called the “Clovis-first” model, was supported by distinctive stone tools found at sites across North America and dated to around 13,000 years ago. For much of the twentieth century, it was treated as established fact.

Challenges to this model have accumulated gradually. In the 1970s and 1980s, archaeologists working at Monte Verde in southern Chile uncovered evidence of human occupation that appeared to predate the Clovis sites by more than a thousand years. The initial reaction from the archaeological community was sceptical, and the findings were fiercely contested. Over time, however, additional excavations and improved dating techniques strengthened the case for Monte Verde, and the site is now widely accepted as evidence that humans reached South America earlier than the Clovis-first model allowed.

The implications of this acceptance are profound. If people were already in southern Chile more than 14,000 years ago, they could not have travelled solely through the ice-free corridor, which is believed to have opened only around 13,000 years ago. This has led researchers to propose alternative routes, most notably a coastal migration along the Pacific shoreline. According to this hypothesis, early groups moved south along the coast, relying on marine resources such as fish, shellfish, and seaweed, rather than waiting for an inland passage to become viable. Because sea levels were lower during the Ice Age, many of the coastal sites that these groups would have occupied are now submerged beneath the ocean, making them extremely difficult to study.

Genetic evidence has provided an independent line of support for a coastal route. Analyses of mitochondrial DNA from modern Indigenous populations suggest that the ancestors of Native Americans diverged from Siberian populations roughly 20,000 to 25,000 years ago, and that they remained isolated for several thousand years before expanding into the Americas. This period of isolation is often referred to as the “Beringian standstill.” The genetic data also indicate that the expansion southward occurred relatively rapidly, perhaps within a few thousand years, which is more consistent with movement along a coastal corridor than with an inland route that would have required adaptation to unfamiliar environments.

Not all researchers, however, accept the coastal hypothesis without reservation. Critics point out that direct archaeological evidence for coastal migration remains sparse. Most of the relevant sites would be underwater, and the few coastal sites that have been investigated have yielded ambiguous results. Some argue that the inland corridor, rather than being a single route, may have functioned as a series of usable passages that opened and closed repeatedly, allowing movement at different times. Others suggest that both routes may have been used, by different groups, at different periods.

What is beyond dispute is that the peopling of the Americas was more complex than the tidy narrative that once dominated textbooks. The timing, the routes, and the number of migrations remain active areas of research, and new discoveries continue to reshape the picture. Rather than a single wave of migrants following a single path, the emerging consensus points to multiple movements, possibly involving different populations, over an extended period. The story of the first Americans is, in other words, still being written.
