The Decline of the Insects
In recent years, a growing number of entomologists have raised concerns that insect populations are undergoing a dramatic decline. The claim is not based on the disappearance of a single charismatic species, but on accumulating evidence from long-term monitoring programmes, museum collections, and the testimonies of amateur naturalists. Across Europe, for example, data from nature reserves suggest that the total biomass of flying insects has fallen substantially over the last three decades. Similar patterns have been reported in parts of North America, Central America, and Australia. Yet despite the breadth of these observations, the issue remains contentious, partly because insect populations are notoriously volatile.

One of the central difficulties in assessing insect decline is the scarcity of reliable baseline data. Insects are extraordinarily diverse, with more than a million described species and perhaps several million more awaiting formal identification. For the vast majority of these, no systematic population records exist. Where data do exist, they are often geographically biased, concentrated in protected areas or in regions with a long tradition of natural history recording. Furthermore, insect numbers can fluctuate dramatically from year to year in response to weather conditions, food availability, and natural cycles. A single bad season, therefore, is not necessarily evidence of long-term decline.

Nevertheless, an increasing number of studies have attempted to control for these fluctuations and still find cause for concern. One influential analysis, published in 2019, reviewed 73 historical reports and concluded that over 40 percent of insect species were experiencing population declines, while only a small fraction were increasing. A third of all species assessed were considered threatened with extinction. The authors identified habitat loss, pesticide use, and climate change as the primary drivers. They also warned that the loss of insects would have cascading consequences for ecosystems, since insects perform essential functions such as pollination, nutrient recycling, and serving as a food source for birds, fish, and mammals.

Not all scientists, however, accept the most alarming interpretations of the data. Some researchers have argued that the existing studies are too geographically limited to support global conclusions. They point out that many regions—particularly in Africa, South America, and parts of Asia—remain almost entirely unstudied. In some of these areas, insect populations may even be increasing as a result of changing land use and climate conditions. A further criticism concerns methodology: studies that rely on biomass measurements, for instance, may be disproportionately influenced by the loss of a few large-bodied species, while increases in smaller species go unnoticed. Thus, while there is broad agreement that certain insect groups are in trouble, the claim of a generalised global collapse is still contested.

The debate has significant implications for conservation policy. If insect decline is as severe as some studies suggest, urgent action is required to protect habitats, restrict harmful pesticides, and reduce carbon emissions. However, if the evidence remains too incomplete to justify alarm, resources might be better directed toward gathering more comprehensive data before implementing costly interventions. Some governments and international bodies have already begun to fund large-scale insect monitoring programmes intended to fill this gap. These initiatives, while less dramatic than emergency conservation measures, reflect a growing recognition that reliable knowledge is a prerequisite for effective action.

There is also a cultural dimension to the problem. Public concern for insects has historically lagged behind that for mammals and birds, in part because insects are often perceived as pests rather than as vital components of ecosystems. This perception influences not only conservation priorities but also the allocation of research funding. A recent survey of entomological research found that a small number of “flagship” insect groups, such as butterflies and bees, receive the vast majority of scientific attention, while other ecologically important orders remain comparatively neglected. This taxonomic bias may distort our understanding of decline, as the insects most studied are not necessarily those most at risk.

Ultimately, the question of whether insects are in the midst of a genuine global crisis, and what should be done about it, is unlikely to be resolved quickly. What is clear is that the current evidence, however incomplete, points to significant changes in insect communities across many parts of the world. Whether these changes constitute a permanent and widespread collapse, or a temporary and localised disturbance, will depend on the accumulation of far more comprehensive data in the years to come.
